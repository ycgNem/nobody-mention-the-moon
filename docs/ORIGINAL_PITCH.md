# Original Pitch (archived)

This is the first version of the idea, kept for reference. It described a **single-player, turn-based puzzle game with progression**.
The game has since been reworked into a **co-op party game you can replay endlessly, with no progression**. See [GDD.md](GDD.md) and [DECISIONS.md](DECISIONS.md).

What we kept from it:
- The title, and the premise: humans hosting monsters who are all pretending to be human.
- Everyone thinks the hosts are legendary monsters. Keeping it that way is the whole job.
- Weaponized small talk: redirect topics, change the subject with props, serve food to interrupt, move seats around.
- Lies become rules you must keep (the Rumor Ledger).
- Guest quirks that link together and that you learn by watching how guests react, not by reading bios.
- Conversation moves around the table by rules you can predict, so you're solving a board that keeps changing, not picking from a dialogue tree.
- Scenario ideas: exes, a monster hunter mistaken for the entertainment, a wedding of two monster families, a birthday for someone who has been dead for 200 years.

What we cut:
- Turn-based play. It's real time now.
- Campaign progression, events you unlock, and persona upgrades between events.
- The single "you". Now 1–6 friends host together.

## Original text

> **Working title: "Nobody Mention the Moon"**
> A turn-based social puzzle game where you host dinner parties for monsters who are all pretending to be human.
> You're the only actual human at the table. Unfortunately, everyone believes you're a legendary monster undercover, and you desperately need to keep it that way.
> Your goal: get through each dinner without anyone revealing their identity, eating another guest, or realizing you're ordinary.
>
> **The main mechanic: weaponized small talk.** Every turn, a guest introduces a conversation topic. You can: redirect it toward another guest; change the subject using something in the room; serve a dish to interrupt or distract someone; rearrange seats under increasingly flimsy excuses; tell a lie that solves an immediate problem but becomes a rule you must maintain.
>
> **Guests have interconnected quirks:** the werewolf gets nervous when someone mentions dogs, and transforms if anyone opens the curtains. The vampire cannot enter a conversation unless invited. The ghost forgets she's dead, but questions about her childhood make objects float. The fae takes every compliment as a binding promise. The shapeshifter copies whoever receives the most attention. You learn these behaviors by watching reactions, not reading everyone's biography upfront.
>
> **What a round looks like:** Someone asks the vampire what he does during the day. You interrupt: "Actually, our host has prepared a lovely garlic soup." Bad move. The vampire panics. You claim garlic is poisonous to you too, preserving his cover, but now you can't eat your own dinner without raising suspicion. The werewolf offers you his steak instead. It's raw. You compliment the fae's necklace to buy time. She replies: "How kind. You may have it in exchange for your name." One tiny conversational rescue has become three new problems.
>
> **What makes it a game rather than a dialogue tree:** conversations move around the table according to predictable rules. Seating, relationships, props, and food affect where a topic travels and how guests respond. A visible rumor ledger tracks everything you've claimed. Guests can connect contradictions, so your invented identity becomes a puzzle you're building against yourself.
>
> **Progression (cut):** start with three guests and a short meal; later events add exes, a monster hunter mistaken for the entertainment, a wedding where neither family knows what the other family is, a surprise birthday for someone dead 200 years. Between events, customize your fictional monster persona.
>
> **The hook:** a strategy game where saying "So, how do you two know each other?" can be more catastrophic than throwing a grenade.
